Product Specification

Otherworld Reads

A quiet, personal reading tracker that helps you find your next book by feel — and sends you to a library or a local bookshop to get it. Not a social network, not a marketplace. A shelf that's yours.

01What this is

Otherworld Reads is a private reading shelf. You keep what you're currently reading — one book, or the two or three you're genuinely alternating between — a TBR pile of what's next, a record of what you've finished and how it felt, and a short list of the libraries and bookshops you actually use.

It already does the first part well. This spec covers what it becomes: a shelf you can dress in a look you like, that knows real books so you never mistype a title, that can suggest something when you only know the mood you're in, and that hands you off to Libby or a local bookshop when you're ready to read it.

The one-sentence test. If a feature doesn't help you (a) remember what you read, (b) decide what to read next, (c) actually get hold of it, or (d) read it alongside someone — it doesn't ship.

Clause (d) is new in version 1.1 and is the one that changed the shape of this document. Reading together (§10), knowing a favourite author has something coming (§11), and authors themselves (§12) are all downstream of it. Everything added under it stays subject to the other three: a club is built around a book you're reading, an announcement is only ever about getting hold of a book, and neither one is allowed to become a feed.

02Principles

Libraries first

Every "get this book" path leads to a library hold or an independent shop before it leads anywhere else. No Amazon links. Availability is shown honestly, including the wait.

Private by default, shared by invitation

Your shelf is yours. No feed, no followers, no public profile — and readers are never searchable. The only thing anyone else sees is a book you deliberately put in a club with them, and even then, only that book. Authors and clubs are findable; the people reading are not.

Feeling over rating

Books get a feeling (💛 Loved / Liked / Not for me) and moods, not a number out of five. Stars invite comparison; feelings invite memory.

Phone-shaped

Designed for one thumb at 11pm. Desktop is the bonus layout, not the baseline. Installable, works offline, opens instantly.

Nothing you must type twice

If a computer can know a book's title, author, cover, and year, you shouldn't be spelling them. Manual entry stays available — it's the fallback, not the default.

Quiet

No streaks, no badges, no yearly challenge counting down at you. Reading is not a productivity metric.

Getting around

Version 1.0 of this spec put every surface in a single phone-length scroll. That worked when there were four of them and the Finished list was short. It does not survive this document: with Current Reads, TBR, Coming Soon, Libraries, Clubs and a Finished list that grows forever, the daily-use surfaces end up buried under an archive.

So the app gets a small amount of navigation — a bottom tab bar on a phone, which is the one navigation pattern an installed PWA can wear without looking like a website in a costume.

Rules

  • The bar grows with the app. Until clubs exist there are two tabs, not three. A tab is never shown leading to a feature that isn't built, and a disabled feature removes its tab entirely rather than greying it out.
  • Cold start always opens Reading. The app does not remember which tab you were on. Opening it should always land you in the same place — that predictability is worth more than restoring state.
  • Within a session, each tab keeps its scroll position. Switching to Finished and back doesn't throw you to the top of a list you were halfway down.
  • No badges, no counts, no dots. An app with no streaks does not get to put a red number on its own navigation. The tab bar is a way to get somewhere, not a reason to.
  • Real URLs. Hash routing — #/finished, #/clubs, #/club/<code> — so the back button behaves, a tab can be linked to, and a club invite is an ordinary link. No build step and no server configuration, which keeps it deployable to GitHub Pages exactly as it is today.
  • Wide screens don't get a bottom bar. Above the breakpoint the tabs move into the header as plain text links and each tab's contents use the existing multi-column grid. Desktop keeps being the bonus layout.

Series

Books arrive in series, and an app that ignores that will keep making the same two mistakes: failing to tell you the sequel to something you loved is coming, and cheerfully offering you book four of something you gave up on in book one. Series is captured when a search result provides it, and drives one rule in each direction.

RuleBehaviour
A loved series is followed A forthcoming book sharing a seriesKey with something you loved is surfaced in Coming Soon (§11) with the connection stated — "book three of the series you loved" rather than an anonymous new title. This is the single most valuable thing that feature can say.
An abandoned series is dropped whole If the earliest book you read in a series was marked not for me — whether you finished it or set it down (§07) — the rest of that series is never suggested by the recommender (§08) and never appears in Coming Soon (§11). One clear "no" should not have to be repeated per book.
"Not right now" does not abandon Setting a book down as not right now is explicitly not a judgment, so it leaves the series fully suggestible. The distinction in §07 exists precisely so this rule can be confident.
Silence when the data is absent Open Library's series data is inconsistent and often missing. Where there is no seriesKey, neither rule applies and nothing is shown — no empty "Series: —" labels, no guessing from title patterns. Series is an enhancement on top of a shelf that works without it.
One shared collection, and only one. Everything above except Club, Membership and AuthorProfile lives inside the reader's own users/{uid} document, exactly as it does today. Those three are the first data in this app that more than one person can read — which is why the security rules for them are treated as a feature in their own right rather than a detail (see Plan, Phase 11).
A deliberate reversal. Cover art was removed from the Current Read in v11 because typing an image URL by hand was tedious and usually skipped. With search-based adding, covers arrive for free from the Open Library CDN — so they come back, but only for books that have one. No placeholder boxes, no broken images: if there's no cover, the card is typographic, exactly as it is today.

05Choosing a vibe

The vibe is the app's whole look — background, palette, typography, texture. It is chosen once and can be changed any time, instantly, from anywhere. It is visual only: every label, every button, every piece of copy stays identical across vibes, so switching never costs you your bearings.

The flow

  1. First launch. Before the shelf appears, a full-screen picker: a grid of vibe cards, each rendering a miniature of the real UI in that vibe (a Current Reads card, a chip, a button). No copy explaining them — you can see them.
  2. Tap one. The whole page transitions into it immediately behind the picker. There's no "confirm" step; the preview is the confirmation.
  3. Enter the shelf. Choice saved. Never asked again.
  4. Change it later. A vibe control in the header — a small palette glyph — opens the same picker. One tap re-skins the app in place. Scroll position, open panels, and any half-typed form are preserved.

The vibes

Otherworld default

Dark fantasy. Near-black with a violet bloom, sea-glass teal and old gold, Cinzel display type. The current look — existing users keep it and are never re-prompted.

Cottage

Warm paper and linen. Cream ground, sage and terracotta, a soft serif. Light-mode, gentle, the reading-nook end of the spectrum.

Celestial

Deep indigo and violet, silver and pale gold, a faint star-field behind the panels. Cool where Otherworld is warm.

Dark Academia

Oxblood, ink, and aged parchment. A proper old-style serif throughout, tight rules, library-card restraint.

Seaglass

Bright and coastal. Soft off-white, muted blue-green, sand. The lightest vibe — the one for reading outdoors.

Design decisions behind the vibe system

DecisionWhy
Visual only, not copy Renaming "The TBR Pile" per vibe is charming once and confusing forever — it breaks muscle memory, complicates search, and doubles the surface to maintain. Deferred, not discarded.
One token file per vibe Every vibe sets the same fixed set of CSS custom properties on :root. Components never reference a vibe by name, so a new vibe is one new file and one line in a registry — never a component edit.
Applied before first paint An inline script in <head> reads the saved vibe and stamps data-vibe on <html> before stylesheets resolve. No flash of the wrong theme on a cold start.
Contrast is non-negotiable Each vibe ships with measured contrast ratios. A vibe that can't hit WCAG AA on body text and interactive elements doesn't ship, however pretty it is.
Theme-color follows the vibe The PWA theme-color meta and the status bar update on switch, so an installed app doesn't have a dark-fantasy notch above a cream page.
Vibe is per-device, then synced Stored locally so it applies instantly offline, and mirrored to the cloud profile so a new device inherits your look instead of reverting to default.

06Finding a book

Today, adding a book means typing a title and an author into two boxes, with nothing checking your spelling. This replaces that with a single search field that suggests real books as you type.

The flow

  1. Tap "+ Add". One field, focused, labelled Title or author.
  2. Type. After a short pause (≈300 ms) and at least 3 characters, results appear beneath — up to 6, each showing cover thumbnail, title, author, and first-published year. Ranked by edition count, so the well-known book sorts above the obscure one with a similar name.
  3. Pick one. The book is added with full metadata. Done — no second screen, no confirm.
  4. Or don't. Below the results sits a permanent Add "what you typed" manually row, which opens the current two-field form. Self-published books, ARCs, a friend's manuscript, and anything Open Library has never heard of all still work.

Behaviour details

CaseWhat happens
OfflineThe search field falls back to manual entry with a quiet note. Adding a book never requires a network.
Slow responseResults are requested with a timeout. Past it, manual entry is offered — the UI never sits on a spinner.
Stale responsesEach keystroke's request supersedes the last; an out-of-order reply is discarded rather than overwriting fresher results.
DuplicateIf the picked book already sits on the TBR or in Finished, the result row says so and offers to jump to it instead of silently creating a second copy.
KeyboardArrow keys move through results, Enter picks, Escape closes. The list is a proper listbox so screen readers announce position and count.
Source: Open Library. Free, no API key, no rate-limit registration, permissive cross-origin access — and it's a project of the Internet Archive rather than a retailer, which suits the ethos. Verified working: a search for piranesi returns title, author, year, and cover ID in a single request.

07Reading & finishing

This flow is already right and changes very little. It's specified here because it's the spine of the app.

  1. Set a current read — from the TBR pile, from search, or with the + Add control on the Current Reads panel itself.
  2. It sits at the top of the app for as long as you're reading it. Title, author, and cover if there is one. Edit and Finish are the only actions.
  3. Finish & rate opens a small modal: How was it? (one of three feelings) and Vibe (any number of mood chips, optional). Both can be skipped.
  4. Save. The book moves to the top of Finished with today's date. Current Read empties and invites the next one.

The vibes captured here are the same vocabulary the AI recommender uses. That's not a coincidence; it's the point. The more you finish, the better it knows your taste. They come in three groups — Atmosphere (Cozy, Dark, Creepy, Dread, Bleak, Whimsical, Hopeful), Story (Epic, Twisty, Slow-burn, Fast-paced, Tense, Funny, Devastating, Romantic, Comfort-read) and Mind (Mind-expanding, Mind-bending, Practical, Dense, Eye-opening) — and you can add your own, because a fixed list picked for fantasy had nothing to say about a horror novel or a textbook. Vibes you type are remembered by being read back off the books that carry them, so the vocabulary never needs its own storage.

More than one at once

Plenty of people read a novel at bedtime, a non-fiction book on the commute, and an audiobook while cooking. The Current Read panel becomes Current Reads — a short list rather than a single slot.

DecisionDetail
Capped at three Enough for the real pattern (fiction / non-fiction / audio), few enough that the panel stays a glance rather than a second TBR pile. Attempting a fourth suggests finishing or shelving one first — it doesn't just refuse. The cap is one constant, easy to revisit once there's evidence.
The panel gains "+ Add" The same search-and-pick flow as §06, so a book can start life as a current read without passing through the TBR pile. This is the affordance the single-slot design never needed.
One is primary The first in the list renders large, with its cover; the others render as compact rows beneath it. Reorder by drag to change which one is the headline. No "primary" flag is stored — position is the ordering, which keeps the data honest.
Finishing is unchanged Each entry has its own Finish button and its own feeling-and-mood modal. Finishing one leaves the others exactly where they were.
Format, optionally An entry may carry a format tag — print, ebook, audio — shown as a small chip. It exists so three simultaneous books are distinguishable at a glance, not as tracking. Always optional, never prompted for.
Empty means empty With nothing current, the panel shows one invitation, not three empty slots.

Setting a book down

Not every book gets finished, and "I stopped reading it" is not the same as "I read it and didn't like it". Collapsing the two loses the more useful fact. So a current read has a third action — Set down — alongside Edit and Finish.

It asks one question, and the two answers mean genuinely different things:

AnswerWhat it meansWhat happens
Not right now Wrong book, wrong week. Nothing is wrong with it and you may well come back. Returns to the TBR pile. Not recorded as an opinion, still suggestible, and it does not abandon the series (§04).
Not for me You gave it a fair go and it isn't going to happen. Goes into the record marked set down, never suggested again, and abandons the series it belongs to.

Feeling and moods stay optional on both — a book you set down at chapter three usually hasn't earned an opinion, and the modal shouldn't demand one. Set-down entries appear in the Finished list with a distinct marker and their own filter, because the record of what didn't work is part of knowing your own taste.

The distinction matters more now that three books can be current at once: the third one is frequently the one that gets abandoned, and without this it either sits there indefinitely or gets misfiled as a book you disliked.

The Finished page

Moving Finished onto its own tab (§04) is what finally lets it be more than a list at the bottom of a scroll. It's the part of the app that gets better every year you use it, and the only screen worth opening for its own sake.

  • Grouped by year, newest first, with a quiet count per year — the one place a number is welcome, because it's a memory rather than a target.
  • Filter by feeling, mood, or set-down — "everything I loved", "everything cozy", "everything I gave up on". The moods captured at finish time already exist; this is the payoff for having captured them.
  • Search across title and author, for when you know you read it but not when.
  • No stats, no charts, no pace. Books per year is a memory; a graph of books per year is a target, and this app doesn't set targets.
Migration. The stored shape moves from current (an object or null) to currentReads (an array). The migration runs on load, is safe to run twice, and is invisible: a reader with one current book sees exactly what they saw before. Old clients that still write current are tolerated for one release, then dropped.

08Reading by vibe

The feature for when you've finished something and don't know what's next. You describe the reading experience you want, in your own words, and get a short list of real books back.

The flow

  1. "Find me something…" — a button on the TBR panel. Opens a sheet with mood chips (the same vocabulary as the finish form) and a free-text field: what are you in the mood for?
  2. Ask. Chips and text are sent along with a compressed picture of your taste: your recent loved books, your most-used moods, and — critically — a list of titles to exclude (everything on your shelf, everything you've already passed on, and every series you've abandoned per §04).
  3. Five suggestions come back, each with a title, an author, and one or two sentences on why this, for you — tied to the mood you asked for and books you've loved. Not a blurb; a reason.
  4. Triage each one, in place. Three actions per card: + TBR Already read Pass. Cards animate out as you decide. "Already read" drops it into Finished with no feeling set, so you can fill that in later. "Pass" remembers it so it's never suggested again.
  5. Ask again if none of them landed. The exclusion list now includes everything you just passed on, so the second round is genuinely different.

How it works

ConcernApproach
Where the key lives Not in the browser. A small serverless function (Cloudflare Worker) holds the Anthropic API key and is the only thing that ever talks to the model. See Plan, Phase 6.
Who can call it Signed-in users only. The browser sends its Firebase ID token; the Worker verifies it and derives the user ID from the token — never from the request body.
How much A per-user daily cap (starting at 10 calls/day). The remaining count is shown in the sheet before you ask, so hitting the limit is never a surprise.
Model claude-opus-5, with structured output so the response is always a valid list of {title, author, why} — no prose parsing, no malformed-response handling in the UI.
How much it knows A compressed taste summary — recent loved books, most-used moods, exclusions — not the full finished list. This is a deliberate starting point rather than a permanent limit: it is cheaper, it keeps far less data leaving the device, and it may simply be good enough. Judge it on the quality of real suggestions before spending more on either count.
Hallucinated books Every suggestion is checked against Open Library before it's shown. Anything that doesn't resolve to a real work is dropped silently. You never see a book that doesn't exist.
Failure Any error — offline, over quota, model unavailable — surfaces as one plain sentence and a Retry, inside the sheet. The rest of the app is unaffected.
Cost, honestly. At roughly 1.5k tokens in and 800 out per request, Opus 5 works out to about 2¢ per recommendation — so a user hitting the full 10/day cap costs about $0.24/day, or ~$7/month. Fine for you and a handful of friends; it does not scale to strangers on the open internet. Two levers exist if it needs to: lower the daily cap, or switch the Worker to Haiku 4.5 (~0.5¢/call, a quarter of the cost). The Worker is one line away from either. Decide when there's a real bill, not before.

09Getting the book

The part that makes this more than a list. Every book on the TBR pile can answer "can I get this right now?" — and the answer is grounded in a real library's collection, not a guess.

Setting up your library

  1. Libraries — now in the Hidden Shelf (§04) — gains an Add library flow that searches actual library systems by name or place, rather than asking for a name and a URL.
  2. Picking one stores its OverDrive key. That key is what unlocks availability.
  3. Name it yourself. The next field is What do you call it?, pre-filled with the library's official name and fully editable. "Mom's card", "the good one", "County" — whatever you'd actually say out loud. That label is what the app shows everywhere afterwards; the official name is kept underneath, in smaller type, only where two entries would otherwise be indistinguishable. Renaming is always available from Edit and never touches the stored key, so a rename can't break availability.
  4. Multiple libraries are supported — many readers have a city card and a county card, which is exactly why the names have to be yours. The first one is treated as primary for availability checks, and the order is yours to change.
  5. Bookshops and plain links are still first-class and named the same way: an entry can be a library, a bookshop, or a bookmark. Only libraries get availability.

On a TBR book

Once a library is set, each TBR entry shows a borrow row:

StateShown asTapping it
Available nowAvailable now · ebook / audiobookOpens the title in Libby, ready to borrow.
On hold~15 day wait · 168 holds on 164 copiesOpens the title in Libby, ready to place a hold.

Feasibility, checked. OverDrive's public catalogue endpoint (the one Libby's own web app uses) returns availability, hold counts, and estimated wait days for a given library, and permits cross-origin requests. A live query against a real library system for Piranesi returned ownedCopies: 164, holdsCount: 168, estimatedWaitDays: 15 — exactly the data this design needs.
And the risk. That endpoint is undocumented and unsupported. It can change or start refusing browser requests without warning. So availability is designed as an enhancement, never a dependency: if the lookup fails for any reason, the borrow row simply doesn't render, and the plain "Search this on Libby" link — which is a normal URL and cannot break — is always present underneath. Every phase of this feature must be shippable with the enhancement switched off.

Buying, when borrowing isn't an option

Where a purchase link is warranted, it goes to Bookshop.org, which splits revenue with independent bookshops, or to a specific local shop the reader has added themselves. There is no Amazon link anywhere in the app, and no affiliate tracking of any kind unless the reader is told about it plainly.

10Reading together

A book club is a small group of people reading one book at the same time. It is the only place in the app where anyone sees anything of yours — and what they see is that book, and nothing else.

This is the app's biggest departure, and it should be treated as one. Everything before this section is a single-player app with a sync backend. A club is genuinely multi-user: shared writes, security rules that matter, other people's words on your screen, and the possibility of someone being unpleasant in a space you built. It ships last, behind everything else, and it is the one feature allowed to be cut on the grounds of "this is more than I want to maintain."

What a club is

PropertyDecision
SmallCapped at twelve members. Small enough that the group is the moderation system; large enough for a real club.
One bookA club reads a single book at a time. Finishing it archives that round; the club can then pick its next book and carry its members forward. No club has a library.
Joined by link, or listedEvery club has an invite link. A club may additionally be listed, making it findable in the club directory by the book it's reading. Listing is a choice the founder makes, and can be undone.
Owned by one personWhoever created it. The owner can remove a member, change the book, and delete the club. There is no second admin tier — at twelve people, hierarchy is overhead.
Named by its membersA club name and nothing else. No description, no rules page, no photo. The book is the identity.

The flow

  1. Start a club from a book — on a TBR entry or a current read, "Read this with people" creates a club around that book and hands you an invite link.
  2. People join. Following the link shows the book, the member count, and one button. Joining copies the book onto their own Current Reads (respecting the cap of three — if they're full, it goes to their TBR and the club still works).
  3. Set a pace, or don't. The owner can add checkpoints — "through chapter 12", "part two" — with optional dates. A club with no checkpoints is a valid club; it just has one implicit checkpoint called finished.
  4. Read, and mark where you are. Each member taps the checkpoint they've reached. That single number is the only progress data in the app, and it exists solely to power the spoiler gate below.
  5. Talk. One flat thread per club. Every message is posted at the checkpoint its author has reached, and is hidden from anyone who hasn't reached it. No replies, no threading, no reactions beyond the feelings below — a club conversation is a conversation, not a forum.
  6. Finish. Marking the book finished in the club finishes it on your own shelf too, through the normal feeling-and-moods modal — one action, both places. Your feeling becomes visible to the club at that moment, and everyone else's becomes visible to you.

Spoiler safety is the whole design

Every reading group in the world has the same failure: someone posts a reaction to chapter 30 and ruins it for the person on chapter 8. The checkpoint model exists to make that structurally impossible rather than a matter of etiquette.

RuleWhy
A message is visible only to members at or past its checkpoint The gate is on read, not on trust. You cannot see a spoiler for a part you haven't reached, even if someone posts one carelessly.
Feelings and moods stay hidden until you finish "Ana loved it" is itself a spoiler. The club shows who has finished, never how they felt, until you've finished too.
Nothing is retro-hidden Moving your checkpoint forward reveals messages; it never conceals ones you've already read. The gate only ever opens.
The gate is enforced server-side Hidden-in-the-UI is not hidden. The security rules make out-of-range messages unreadable, so a determined member with the console sees no more than a polite one does.

What a club can and cannot see

Members see

  • The club's book
  • Each member's display name
  • Which checkpoint each member has reached
  • Messages at or below their own checkpoint
  • Feelings and moods — only once they've finished

Members never see

  • Your shelf, TBR, or finished list
  • Anything about your other current reads
  • Your libraries, your vibe, your email
  • Your other clubs
  • Anything at all after you leave

Leaving, removing, and ending

ActionWhat happens
You leave a clubYou're removed immediately. You're offered leave or leave and delete my messages — the choice is yours, not the club's. The book stays on your shelf; it's yours now.
The owner removes youSame as leaving, minus the choice — your messages remain unless you'd already deleted them. You lose access immediately and are not told a reason the app invented.
You block a memberTheir messages are hidden from you everywhere, in every club. Blocking is per-reader and needs no one's permission.
The owner deletes the clubThe club and every message in it are gone. Each member's own shelf is untouched.
The club goes quietNothing. There are no nudges, no "your club misses you", no streak. A club that stops is allowed to stop.
Moderation, honestly. At twelve invited people there is no moderation queue and no report button that reaches a human, because there is no human on the other end. The safety model is: clubs are small, joining is by invitation or explicit choice, the owner can remove anyone, and any reader can block anyone permanently. That is genuinely enough for friends reading a book together, and genuinely not enough for a public platform — which is precisely why readers are never searchable and why club listing is opt-in. If clubs ever grow beyond that, this section needs rewriting before the feature does.

11Coming soon

The moment a favourite author announces a new book is one of the few genuinely delightful pieces of news a reading app can deliver. Today you find out by accident, months late. This is the fix.

The flow

  1. Watch an author. Any author already in your library — finished, current, or TBR — has a Watch control on their name. One tap, no configuration.
  2. Or be offered it. When you finish a book and mark it 💛 Loved, the finish modal quietly offers to watch that author. Offered once per author, never nagged.
  3. A "Coming soon" panel appears — only once you're watching someone and something is actually coming. It lists forthcoming titles by release date, nearest first, each with the author, the date, and how confident that date is.
  4. Act on it. Each entry offers + TBR (so it's waiting for you), place a hold where your library already lists the title as on order, and preorder from Bookshop.org or a shop you've added. Library first, exactly as in §09.
  5. It ages out. On release day the entry moves to your TBR if you added it, and otherwise quietly disappears. Nothing accumulates.

Where the data comes from — and the honest problem

This is the hardest feature in the spec to source, because catalogues are built to describe books that exist. Forthcoming titles are patchy everywhere, and no single source is sufficient.

SourceGivesConfidence
Open Library Author identity and their known works — the anchor for "is this new to me?" Good for identity, poor for unreleased titles
Google Books Volumes carrying a future publishedDate, searchable by author The best free source for dates, inconsistent coverage
OverDrive / Libby Titles a specific library has on order, with an expected availability date Strong where it exists — and it's the library-first answer
The author A verified author announcing their own forthcoming book (§12) Highest — the author knows. Covers indie and small-press books no catalogue indexes
Unverified, and stated as such. Unlike the Open Library search and the OverDrive availability endpoint — both of which were checked against live data before being written into this spec — the forthcoming-title behaviour of these sources has not been verified. It must be, with real queries against real authors, before any of Phase 8 is built. If the coverage turns out to be too thin to be useful, the honest outcome is to ship the author-announced path (§12) alone and drop the catalogue crawl entirely.

Behaviour details

ConcernApproach
When it checks On app open, at most once per author per day, staggered across authors so a reader watching thirty people doesn't fire thirty requests on a cold start. Results cached with the release entry.
No server needed Version one runs entirely in the browser against public endpoints. If a nightly server-side sweep is ever wanted, the Worker from §08 is the place for it — but it is not required for the feature to work.
Notifications Off. The default is a quiet panel you find when you open the app. Push notification is strictly opt-in, per-reader, and — on iOS — only possible once the app is installed to the home screen. An app with no streaks and no badges does not get to start pushing.
Series The best thing this panel can say is "the next one is coming". A forthcoming book in a series you loved is labelled with that connection rather than appearing as an unfamiliar title; a series you abandoned never appears at all. Both rules are defined once in §04.
False positives Re-issues, box sets, foreign editions and audiobook re-releases all look like new books to a catalogue. Anything whose title closely matches a work already on your shelf is suppressed. A wrong entry is worse than a missing one.
Dates that move Publication dates slip constantly. A date is shown with its precision — "March 2027" rather than a false "4 March 2027" — and updates silently when the source changes.
Failure The panel doesn't render. No error, no empty state explaining a broken API. Same rule as availability in §09: an enhancement, never a dependency.

12Authors

An author is a reader who has additionally proven they wrote something. Same account, same shelf, same sign-in — one extra, verified role that unlocks exactly two things: announcing their own forthcoming books, and hosting a club for one of them.

Why this earns its place. It isn't a marketing surface bolted onto a reading app. It's the fix for §11's weakest link: the one entity that reliably knows a book is coming, months before any catalogue does, is the person writing it. For indie and small-press authors — precisely the ones a library-first, no-Amazon app should serve — the author is the only source. Everything else here follows from keeping that channel trustworthy.

What an author can do

Claim their name

Link a verified identity to an author key, so the books already on readers' shelves connect to a real person.

Announce a forthcoming book

Title, release date, cover, and where to preorder — including a library link where one exists. This appears in the Coming Soon panel of readers already watching them.

Host one club later

A read-along for one of their own books, with the author actually present in the thread. Same twelve-person cap, same checkpoint rules, no special powers in the conversation. Arrives with clubs themselves (§10), which is the last thing built — authors work fully without it.

Correct their own metadata

Fix a wrong cover, a mangled series order, a misattributed title — for their books only, and visible only inside this app.

What an author cannot do — the load-bearing half

Not possibleWhy
Reach a reader who hasn't opted in An announcement is delivered only to readers who chose to watch that author. There is no broadcast, no "authors you might like", no announcement in anyone's shelf uninvited. Watching is the entire permission model.
Learn who their readers are — or how many No names, no shelves, no emails, no export, and no count either. A watcher count was considered and deliberately left unbuilt: it would be the only metric in an app that has none, and no author has asked for one. It waits for a real author to want it, for a better reason than "other platforms have one". If it ever ships it is aggregate only, and withheld below a threshold so an author with three watchers cannot deduce who they are from who they invited.
Message readers There is no author-to-reader channel. The only place an author writes to readers is inside their own club, where those readers chose to be, alongside them.
Pay for anything No promoted placement, no boosted announcements, no paid inclusion in AI recommendations, ever. The recommender is told nothing about who is an author. If money could move a book up a list, every list in the app becomes worthless.
Announce someone else's book Announcements are scoped to the author key they verified against. A publisher wanting to announce a catalogue is out of scope — and stays out of scope.

Verification

Verification is the whole feature. An unverified author claim is worse than no author feature at all, because it converts "the author says a book is coming" from the highest-confidence source in §11 into an impersonation vector. So claiming is gated, and an unverified claim stores nothing and displays nothing.

MethodHow it worksSuits
Domain Sign in with an email at the author's own domain, or place a token file at a URL on their site — the standard proof-of-control pattern, entirely automatic. Authors with their own site
Publisher referral A one-time link issued to a publisher or agent who confirms the author. Traditionally published authors
Manual review A human — at this scale, you — checks a claim against a public author page and approves it. Everyone else, initially
Manual review does not scale, and that's fine — for now. At the size this app is built for, a handful of claims a month is a few minutes of work and produces a better outcome than any automated check. It is, however, the first thing that breaks if the app grows, and it must not be pretended otherwise. Two honest outcomes when it does: close claiming to domain-verified authors only, or stop accepting new claims until there's a real process. Never lower the bar to keep up with volume — an unverified badge is a lie the app is telling its readers.

Reader-side controls

  • Watching is the only switch, and it's yours. Unwatch and that author can never reach you again.
  • Author announcements are labelled as such — visibly distinct from a catalogue-sourced release, so you always know whether a date came from a database or from the person writing the book.
  • An author's announcement carries no more weight in the UI than a catalogue entry, beyond that label. It doesn't jump the queue or render larger.
  • You can turn the whole thing off. A single setting hides authors, watches, and the Coming Soon panel entirely, returning the app to exactly what it is without them.

13Data & privacy

QuestionAnswer
Where does my shelf live?In your browser when signed out. In your own document in Firestore when signed in, readable and writable only by you.
Can anyone else see it?Your shelf, no — there is no public URL, no feed, and readers are not searchable. The single exception is a club you joined, where the members you invited or joined see that club's book and your progress in it. Nothing else on your shelf is ever readable by another person.
What does a club member learn about me?Your display name, which checkpoint of that one book you've reached, your messages in that club, and — only after you finish — your feeling and moods for that book. Not your other current reads, not your TBR, not your finished list, not your other clubs.
Does an author know I'm watching them?No — and at first they don't even see how many. The watcher count isn't being built (§12). Watching is stored on your own document; the author's record is not written to when you watch, so there is nothing on their side to read.
What leaves the device?Book searches go to Open Library. Availability checks go to OverDrive with a library key and a title — no account, no card number, no identity. Release checks go to Google Books and Open Library with an author name. AI requests go to the Worker with your moods, a short taste summary, and your sign-in token.
Does the AI see my whole shelf?No — a compressed summary: recent loved titles, frequent moods, and an exclusion list. Not your full history, not your dates, not your notes.
Is anything logged?The Worker logs a per-user call count for rate limiting and nothing else. No prompts, no results, no titles.
Is there any advertising or tracking?No analytics, no ad network, no third-party script of any kind. Where a purchase link earns a commission, the page says so in plain language at the point of the link — see §15.
Can I leave?Export to JSON, and delete-everything, are both first-class actions. Leaving should be as easy as arriving. Deleting your account removes you from every club and deletes your messages.

14Accessibility & performance

Targets

  • WCAG AA contrast in every vibe, verified per release
  • Every action reachable by keyboard; visible focus rings
  • Modals and the Hidden Shelf trap focus and restore it on close
  • 16px minimum on inputs (no iOS zoom-on-focus)
  • 44px minimum tap targets, tab bar included
  • The tab bar is a nav landmark; the active tab is marked aria-current
  • Changing tabs moves focus to the new view's heading and announces it — a router that only repaints is invisible to a screen reader
  • The bar clears the iOS home indicator via env(safe-area-inset-bottom), and never covers the last row of a list
  • Live regions announce shelf changes
  • prefers-reduced-motion honoured for all transitions

Staying current

  • Updates arrive on their own — no prompt, no gesture, nothing to know
  • Checked on launch and on every return to the foreground
  • Applied on the next reload, once, never in a loop
  • Never applied over someone mid-input
  • The version is shown as a label, not a button

Budgets

  • Interactive in under 1s on a mid-range phone, cold
  • Full offline function for everything except search, availability, and AI
  • Under 150 KB of JavaScript, uncompressed, excluding the Firebase SDK
  • No layout shift on load — the vibe applies before first paint
  • No blocking spinner anywhere; every remote call has a timeout and a fallback

How updates reach people

A reader should never have to know this app has versions. There is no "update available" banner, no changelog to dismiss, and nothing to tap: a new version is fetched in the background and applied the next time the app is opened or brought back to the foreground.

This already works today and is not new — the service worker takes over immediately on install rather than waiting for every tab to close, which is the step most PWAs get wrong and the reason so many of them feel permanently out of date. The one thing being removed is a hidden tap-the-version-to-check gesture, which duplicated behaviour that happens on its own.

RuleWhy
Never reload over someone An update that arrives while you're typing a title into a modal and throws the text away is worse than an update that waits ten seconds. If a modal is open or a field has content, the new version is held and applied at the next quiet moment.
Once, never in a loop A reload triggered by a worker taking control must be guarded, or a bad state becomes an app that reloads forever. It fires once per update and never on first install.
The version stays visible As a plain label in the footer, not a button. It is the first thing worth knowing when something looks wrong — but reading it is all anyone should ever need to do with it.
Offline is unaffected An update check that fails changes nothing. The cached app keeps working, and the new version arrives whenever the network next does.
The cost of this convenience. Automatic updates mean a broken release reaches every reader within a minute of being pushed, and there is no way for anyone to stay on the version that worked. That is a genuine trade, made deliberately: the alternative is readers stranded on old versions with no idea why the app is misbehaving. It is also exactly why testing locally before deploying is a hard rule (Plan, Phase 0) rather than a preference — with auto-update, "push it and see" is a decision about everyone's app, not just yours. The rollback path is the same as the release path: revert the commit, bump the cache name, push.

15Keeping it running

The good news first: this app is nearly free to run, and that is a design achievement rather than an accident. A static site on GitHub Pages, a database with a generous free tier, and one serverless function means the fixed cost of Otherworld Reads existing is about zero. Only one thing costs real money per user, and it's the AI.

What it actually costs

ComponentCostFree-tier ceiling
Hosting (GitHub Pages)$0Effectively unlimited for a static site
Database (Firestore, Spark plan)$0~50k reads and ~20k writes per day, 1 GiB stored. A shelf is a single small document — hundreds of readers stay comfortably inside this. Clubs are the first feature that meaningfully increases writes.
Worker (Cloudflare, free plan)$0~100k requests/day — orders of magnitude more than this will ever need
Domain (optional)~$12/yearOnly if you want a name that isn't github.io
AI recommendations~2.8¢ per request No free tier. This is the entire variable cost of the app.
The whole problem in one number. At roughly 1.5k tokens in and 800 out, Claude Opus 5 ($5 per million input, $25 per million output) works out to about 2.8¢ per recommendation. A reader using the full 10/day allowance costs about 28¢ a day — roughly $8.50 a month. Ten such readers is $85/month. That is the number every option below is trying to solve, and switching the Worker to Haiku 4.5 ($1/$5 per million) drops it to about 0.55¢ per request — a fifth of the cost, or roughly $1.70/month for the same heavy reader. One line in the Worker.

The options, judged

OptionVerdictReasoning
Donations
Ko-fi, GitHub Sponsors, Buy Me a Coffee		 Yes — primary Fits the ethos exactly, costs nothing to add, and asks nothing of people who can't pay. Ko-fi takes no platform cut on one-off tips and GitHub Sponsors charges individuals no fee, so nearly all of a donation arrives (payment processing still applies). It won't cover a large bill and shouldn't be expected to — it covers a domain and a modest API spend, which is exactly the size of this problem.
Bookshop.org affiliate Yes — secondary The app already links to Bookshop.org on principle; their affiliate programme pays a commission on sales (commonly cited around 10% — verify the current rate before relying on it) and splits revenue with independent bookshops. It earns only when the app does its actual job. Non-negotiable condition: §09 already promises no affiliate tracking unless the reader is told plainly — so the disclosure ships in the same release as the link, at the point of the link, not buried in a policy page.
Libro.fm affiliate (audiobooks) Worth a look The audiobook equivalent, also independent-bookshop-aligned. Same disclosure rule. Only relevant once the format tag from §07 exists to know an audiobook is wanted.
Bring your own key Yes — ship early A reader pastes their own Anthropic API key and gets uncapped recommendations at their own expense. Costs you nothing, needs no payment infrastructure, and removes the cost ceiling entirely for the technical few. Useless to a non-technical friend — which is fine, because it's a release valve, not the plan.
One-time supporter unlock Only if needed A single payment that permanently raises your daily AI cap. Not a subscription — you pay once and keep it. The honest flaw: a one-time payment against a recurring cost eventually loses, so the raised cap must stay capped, not become unlimited. Build only when there's a real bill.
Advertising No Four independent reasons, any one of which is sufficient. It requires third-party tracking scripts, which contradicts every promise in §13. It adds weight and blocks the performance budget in §14. At a few thousand page views a month it pays single-digit dollars — less than the donations it would displace. And book ads come from exactly the retailers §09 refuses to link to. It's not a close call.
Subscriptions No — ruled out Explicitly off the table. Recorded here as a decision so it doesn't get relitigated every time the bill arrives. It also carries a real obligation this project shouldn't take on: a subscription is a promise of indefinite maintenance to strangers.
Charging authors No The obvious idea once §12 exists, and the one that would quietly ruin it. The moment an author pays for reach, every list in the app is suspect and §12's "cannot pay for anything" rule collapses. Authors are a reason the app is worth using, not a revenue line.

Cost control is the real lever

Revenue is the smaller half of this. The app is cheap enough that not spending solves the problem more reliably than earning does:

  • The daily cap already exists (§08) and is the single biggest control. Lowering it from 10 to 5 halves the worst case immediately.
  • A monthly budget ceiling with a graceful off-switch. When the month's AI spend hits a set figure, the feature says so plainly — "the recommender is resting until the 1st" — and the rest of the app carries on untouched. A known, boring limit beats a surprise invoice.
  • The model swap is one line. Haiku 4.5 at roughly a fifth the cost is a config change, not a rewrite. Try it on real prompts before assuming the quality difference matters for this task.
  • Growth is invitation-shaped. Readers aren't searchable and there's no feed, so the app grows by people sending it to people. That caps cost by design — an accidental side effect of the privacy model that happens to be the best cost control in the list.
  • Set a billing alert on the Anthropic account before the first person who isn't you touches the feature. Not a suggestion.
The recommendation. Ship a quiet donation link and bring-your-own-key first — both are an afternoon's work and neither changes the product. Add the Bookshop.org affiliate with its disclosure when the purchase links land. Keep the daily cap and add the monthly ceiling. Revisit only when there is an actual bill to look at, and never make the app worse to fund the app.

16Decisions & non-goals

DecisionRationale
No build step Native ES modules and plain CSS, served straight from GitHub Pages. Deploying stays git push, and the project can be picked up after six months away without fighting a toolchain.
Static frontend + one serverless function The only thing that genuinely needs a server is holding the AI key. Everything else the browser can do itself. A single Worker beats a whole backend to maintain.
Enhancements degrade, never block Search, availability, and AI are all optional layers over a shelf that works offline with nothing but typing. Any one of them can fail and the app is still the app.
Vibes are visual only (for now) Ships in a fraction of the time, and keeps every label stable while the app is still growing.
Open Library over Google Books No key, no quota registration, and a non-commercial custodian. Better fit for a library-first app.
Suggestions are verified before display A recommender that invents books is worse than no recommender. Cross-checking costs one request and buys complete trust in the list.
A tab bar, not a longer scroll Daily-use surfaces and a permanent archive are different activities and stop fitting on one screen the moment the archive grows. Hash routing buys real URLs, a working back button, and linkable club invites without a build step or any server configuration.
Configuration lives behind the gear Libraries, vibe and account are set once and left alone. Keeping them off the reading screen costs nothing, because the library's actual value shows up on the books themselves.
Sharing is a place, not a state Clubs are the only shared surface, and you are either in one or you are not. There is no partial visibility, no "friends of friends", no setting whose meaning you have to remember. Readers stay unsearchable; only clubs and authors are findable.
Author is a role, not a product One verified flag on an ordinary account, unlocking two things. Every author capability is opt-in on the reader's side, so the reader experience is identical whether or not authors exist.
No advertising, and no paid placement of any kind Both would require breaking a promise made elsewhere in this spec — tracking scripts against §13, third-party weight against §14, retailer money against §09. Funded by donations, affiliate commission on links the app would make anyway, and keeping costs near zero.
No subscriptions A deliberate constraint, not an oversight. It also avoids promising strangers indefinite maintenance of a personal project.

Explicitly not building

  • A social network — no feeds, no followers, no public shelves, no reader search, no profiles. Clubs are a room you're invited into, not a network you join.
  • Direct messaging — the only place anyone writes to anyone is a club thread about the book that club is reading.
  • Author-to-reader broadcast — an author reaches only readers who chose to watch them, and never learns who they are.
  • Ads, sponsored placement, or paid ranking — see §15.
  • Reading challenges, streaks, or stats dashboards — counting is not the point.
  • Progress tracking (page counts, % complete) — a book is either current or it isn't.
  • Amazon or Goodreads integration — against the premise.
  • User-authored custom vibes — deferred; a theme editor is its own product.
  • Native apps — the PWA installs to the home screen and that's enough.

17Open questions

Several questions from earlier versions are now answered and have moved into the spec proper: multiple current reads (§07), the shared-shelf question resolved into clubs (§10), a did-not-finish state (§07), series tracking and the abandoned-series rule (§04), the AI starting from a taste summary rather than the full list (§08), and the donation link living in Settings and nowhere else (§15).

QuestionLeaning
Does Coming Soon belong on the Reading tab, or somewhere of its own? On Reading for now, because it's short and it feeds the TBR pile directly. If it ever grows past a few entries it's competing with the books you're actually reading, and it should move.
Should a club with unread messages be able to signal that on the tab bar? Leaning no, and the "no badges" rule in §04 currently forbids it. But a club conversation nobody knows is happening is a club that dies quietly — this is the one place the rule might genuinely be wrong. Revisit with a real club, not in the abstract.
Can a club read something other than a book — a series, or "whatever we pick monthly"? Probably a series eventually, since that's how real clubs behave. But one book at a time is what makes the checkpoint model coherent, so this needs care rather than enthusiasm.
What happens when a club's owner disappears? Unresolved, and it will happen. Options: the club freezes, ownership passes to the longest-standing member, or any member can dissolve it. Leaning toward automatic transfer after a long inactivity window — but it needs deciding before clubs ship, not after.
Should checkpoints be per-club or per-book? Per-club is specified, because a club's owner knows their group. Per-book (shared chapter markers, created once and reused) would be less repetitive work but requires trusted shared metadata the app doesn't have. Revisit if the same book is clubbed repeatedly.
Does an author need a watcher count at all? Not at first — it isn't being built. It would be the only metric in an app that has none, and no author has asked for it yet. It waits for a real author to say they need it, and for a reason better than "other platforms have one". Nothing depends on it, so adding it later is cheap. If it ever arrives: aggregate only, never names, and withheld below a threshold so a small following can't be deduced from who was invited.
What happens to a club when the author hosting it stops showing up? Nothing special, ideally — an author-hosted club should degrade into an ordinary club rather than dying. Needs confirming once §12 exists.
